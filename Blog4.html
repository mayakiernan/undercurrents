Which states make insurers knock money off your premium for a stronger roof, which are about to, and where PACE financing can cover the upgrade up front.

A FORTIFIED roof, ember-proof vents or hurricane shutters cost money today and pay off on a bad day years from now. Two kinds of policy try to close that gap.

01Mitigation discounts

A handful of states require insurers to cut the premium when a home is built or retrofitted to a recognized standard, usually IBHS FORTIFIED for wind and hail, or a state list of wildfire hardening steps. Elsewhere, discounts are up to each carrier, and they can be small or missing.

02PACE financing

Property Assessed Clean Energy loans pay for upgrades up front and are repaid through the property-tax bill over as long as 20 or 30 years. Most states allow it for commercial buildings (C-PACE). Only California and Florida still run it for homes, and a growing number count storm resilience as eligible.

03Where they meet

A discount lowers the yearly cost of insurance; PACE removes the upfront cost of the work. Where a state has both, the upgrade can start paying for itself in the first year. Switch the map to Both to see how few states that is.

Notes & sources

This is a research snapshot as of November 2026, written for a general reader, not legal advice. State insurance rules change every session, and PACE programs launch and pause often. Where sources disagree, the state's detail panel says so. Check with the state insurance department or PACENation before acting on any one entry.

“Required” means a statute or regulation obliges insurers to give a discount or rate reduction for qualifying mitigation. Most of these laws let insurers set the size of the discount from their own data; Louisiana and Minnesota now publish benchmark levels. “In the works / partial” covers pending bills, rules not yet in force, regulators’ directives, and states whose status sources dispute. PACE counts for operating programs vary by tracker (32 to 36 states plus D.C. in 2026); “Law passed, not yet running” marks states with an enabling law but no active program.
